## Suggested rhetorical structure

### 1. Show the machine — 100–150 words

A long spacecraft skims the atmosphere, compresses about **6.19 tonnes of air per
orbit**, and deploys its heat stores in space. Connect to the
[original concept](https://alancoding.github.io/concepts/atmospheric-scoop-divers/).
This is a numerical engineering reference, not a flight-ready spacecraft. Use the
scale drawing and orbit figure. The open inlet, thermal storage and coast recovery
make the operating sequence understandable before the component details.

### 1a. What is inherited, and what is different — about 250 words

**Editorial update:** use the [four-figure break-out article](ELLIPTICAL_COLLECTION_ORBITS.md)
for the expanded comparison. Slough's newly supplied **2022 RON paper** explicitly
includes repeated elliptical collection, batteries and perigee reboost. The
2026 *Space Gas Station* discussed below is a separate design; the original
two-source scope is not a basis for a broader novelty claim.

Suggested prose, with the detailed comparison kept behind a link:

Atmospheric collection is not a new idea, and neither is compressing the gas in
stages. Michael Minovitch's [self-refueling spacecraft patent](https://patents.justia.com/patent/4754601),
filed in 1984 and granted in 1988, describes a diffuser, temporary hot-gas
storage, serial turbocompressors with cooling between them, and cryogenic
products. It explicitly allows liquefaction to continue after the atmospheric
pass. Retractable radiators and using collected nitrogen for propulsion are
also present. Those are precedents, not discoveries of this project.

John Slough's [*The Space Gas Station*](https://msnw.com/?page_id=26), an
author-hosted preprint, explores a different operating regime: a lightweight
collector around 300 km, freezing oxygen onto cryogenic surfaces and using
ambient ions for drag compensation. It also discusses elliptical collection
orbits, dawn–dusk sun synchronization, and the time to collect the spacecraft's
own mass. The mass-payoff metric is therefore shared, too.

Slough's earlier **2022 Refueling Orbital Navigator** is an even closer mission
precedent: it explicitly combines repeated elliptical collection, multi-stage
pumping, compressed-gas storage and battery-backed perigee thrust. Those ideas
also belong in the inherited foundation, as the [break-out article](ELLIPTICAL_COLLECTION_ORBITS.md)
illustrates. It must not be confused with the later cryogenic/ambient-ion design.

The distinction worth investigating here is the thermal cycle. In this v1.0.1
design, solid plates inside successive compression volumes absorb heat during
a brief, much deeper dive. Those same attached plates then deploy into space
to radiate their stored heat during coast. They are finite heat stores as well
as heat-transfer surfaces. Neither supplied source describes that particular
arrangement. Slough's cold surfaces instead accumulate frozen product; the
patent's staged cooling uses space radiators along the gas path.

Our contribution is a specific implementation and a reproducible,
time-dependent sizing study, including thermal and electrical cycle closure.
That is a narrower claim than inventing atmospheric refueling—and a more useful
one to test. A [source-by-source comparison](LITERATURE_COMPARISON.md) records
the overlaps, distinctions and unresolved assumptions; two documents cannot
establish historical priority.

### 2. Explain the thermal cycle — 200–250 words

Five finite cooling volumes and four dive compressor blocks carry gas toward the
main receiver. Front SiC and downstream aluminum plates hold heat temporarily;
the same attached surfaces deploy to radiate it during coast. Receiver plates
remain a part of v1.0.1. Hatches open only after their chambers have drawn down;
the inlet has no door. The future fixed-coolant architecture is a different version.

Explain the four staggered rows and the explicit effective thermal-spacing
approximation. Wider physical channels reduce the charged friction cost, while
the adopted offset model retains a smaller effective thermal spacing. This is a
reduced engineering assumption requiring validation, not measured exchanger data.
Link the [rack table and equations](../../investigations/valve_reserve/README.md)
instead of filling the article with every dimension.

### 3. Tour the storage and propulsion — 150–200 words

The four forward compressor blocks, coolers and hatch allowances span about
**48.1 m**. The **143.9 m main receiver** ends at x=192 m, followed by the
**125 m full-bore reserve**. Folded solar panels, cryogenic equipment and other
packages narrow the main receiver to about **25.90 m diameter**. Aft thrusters
are external; their mass is included, their bodies do not displace tank volume.

The reserve supplies approach thrust, opens when main-receiver pressure catches
it, and isolates before coast product processing. There is no reserve booster.
Propellant bypasses liquefaction. The main receiver supplies coast compressors
and the liquefaction train; liquid accumulates in separate product storage.
Do not claim a new CM or aerodynamic stability result from this scale drawing.

### 4. State the payoff and its boundary — 250–300 words

**306.4 days** of modeled production equals the dry mass, with **3.086 tonnes of
new useful liquid per orbit**. The largest masses are solar, batteries, propulsion,
electrical distribution and contingency. The 453-tonne solid coolers are the
thermal enabler, not the majority of dry mass.

    payoff time = dry mass / new useful liquid per orbit × orbit period

The endpoint is liquid aboard the operating elliptical orbit. Circular-apogee
export/return is not yet modeled. Describe the constrained solve in one sentence:
46 scalar coordinates, successful local COBYLA termination and an independent
tighter-resolution replay. Do not call it a global optimum. The period repeats
with closure of reserve, thermal and electrical states.

Capture is **99.9787% of modeled incident supply**; all commanded thrust is supplied.
Minimum reserve inventory is 2.07 tonnes. Low-pressure feed acceptance is still an
engineering assumption. These are stronger accounting checks than a simple mass
budget, but they do not resolve inlet shock physics or validate machinery.

If discussing previous numbers, link the [comparison](../../investigations/publication_v1_0_1/README.md):
306.4 is 30.7 days above the rejected 275.73-day boosted candidate, or 28.4 days above
the original 278.02-day snapshot. It has less dry mass but less product per cycle.
Do not imply that the entire difference is a controlled measurement of one change.

### 5. End at this version boundary — 100–150 words

v1.0.1 freezes a reproducible design that can be illustrated and challenged. The
largest uncertainties remain compressor capability, inlet/leading-edge survival,
low-pressure thruster feed, thermal/deployment engineering and orbital delivery.
The next methodological step is a transparent sizing worksheet, subsystem studies
and transient validation, as outlined in the [staged plan](../../modeling/optimization/STAGED_DESIGN_PLAN.md).
That is a plan, not a new numerical result. v1.1 coolant tubes and v2.0 mothership
architecture remain separate future versions. Further visual modeling can follow.
